Total Estimated Number of Galaxies

In 1934 Edwin Hubble counted about 44,000 galaxies on 1,283 photographic plates and treated that as a fair sample of the cosmos.1 In 2025 a single JWST catalogue listed 784,016 sources in a patch of sky only about 2.7 times the size of the full Moon.37 Between those two numbers sits one of astronomy's most quoted and least understood figures: the number of galaxies in the observable universe.

The short answer · November 2026

Somewhere between a few hundred billion and about two trillion, and the spread is mostly about definition, not measurement. Telescopes at Hubble depth would see roughly 250 billion galaxies across the whole sky.7 The two-trillion figure adds the faint galaxies that models say must exist down to a million solar masses in stars.7 No one has directly seen most of them, and no paper published in 2025 or 2026 has replaced that estimate.

A careful one-sentence version reads: “about 2 × 10¹² galaxies with stellar mass ≥ 10⁶ M☉ out to redshift 8, of which roughly a tenth are detectable today.”

A Century of Counting

Every estimate since Hubble works the same way. Astronomers count galaxies in a small patch of sky, then scale the count up to the whole sky, about 41,253 square degrees. Each generation of telescope has gone fainter and found more.

  1. 1934 · Mount WilsonHubble counts ~44,000 galaxies over 650 deg² of photographic plates and finds them spread evenly across the sky.1
  2. 1995 · Hubble Deep FieldA team points Hubble at an apparently empty patch near the Big Dipper for 150 orbits and 342 exposures. About 3,000 galaxies appear in roughly 5 arcmin².2 A southern counterpart in 1998 finds about 2,500 more.3
  3. 2004 · Hubble Ultra Deep FieldOne million seconds of exposure on 11 arcmin² reveal at least 10,000 objects, about 1/13,000,000 of the sky.4
  4. 2012 · eXtreme Deep FieldTwo million seconds and more than 2,000 images. NASA quotes about 5,500 galaxies, while the team's catalogue lists 7,121 in the deepest region. The difference comes from detection thresholds, which is a small reminder that even counting is a choice.5
  5. 2016 · Two trillionConselice and colleagues combine deep-field mass functions out to z = 8 and conclude the universe holds about ten times more galaxies than the 200-billion figure then in use.7,8
  6. 2019 · Hubble Legacy Field7,500 exposures taken over 16 years are stitched into one view containing 265,000 galaxies.6
  7. 2021–2024 · New HorizonsMeasurements of background light from beyond Pluto at first appear to challenge the trillions, then end up agreeing with known galaxies.16,19 The full story is told below.
  8. 2022–2026 · JWSTCEERS catalogues more than 80,000 galaxies in ~100 arcmin².35 JADES DR5 has ~500,000 in 469 arcmin², including 2,081 beyond z = 8.36 COSMOS-Web's COSMOS2025 lists 784,016 sources in 0.54 deg².37
  9. 2025–2026 · The wide-field eraEuclid's first quick release spots 26 million galaxies in 63 deg²,38 and Rubin's first image holds ~10 million.40 The LSST begins on 30 June 2026,41 Roman launches on 30 August 2026,43 and Euclid's first large data release is scheduled for 12 November 2026.39

The arithmetic behind the headline numbers fits in a few lines. Pick a deep field below and the calculator scales its count to the full sky, which is about 148.5 million square arcminutes. Then add the galaxies that field is too shallow to detect.

Caveats: a single small field can sit in a crowded or sparse region (cosmic variance). JWST catalogues include some stars and duplicate detections. Professional estimates integrate mass functions over volume and redshift rather than scaling one image.

Key Fact — Hubble Ultra Deep Field

The HUDF image was assembled from a ~1-million-second exposure in 2003–2004 and revealed approximately 10,000 galaxies in a patch of sky covering roughly 1/13,000,000 of the total sky area.4 Scale that count up and you get about 135 billion galaxies, which is the origin of the familiar “one to two hundred billion.” Conselice's re-analysis of the same depth gives about 250 billion detectable galaxies across the whole sky.7

Inside the Two-Trillion Figure

The 2016 study (Conselice, Wilkinson, Duncan & Mortlock, The Astrophysical Journal 830, 83) did not count two trillion objects.7 It took galaxy stellar mass functions measured in deep fields from z ≈ 0.2 to 8, which describe how many galaxies there are of each mass per unit volume. It fitted each one with a Schechter function, extended the fits down to a stellar mass of 10⁶ M☉, and integrated over the cosmic volume in our past light cone. The headline result is 2.0 (+0.7/−0.6) × 10¹² galaxies at z < 8. Two alternative ways of doing the integral bracket it at 1.2 × 10¹² and 2.8 × 10¹².7

Three details are usually lost in retelling:

  • The answer depends on the low-mass cutoff. Moving the floor from 10⁶ to 10⁵ M☉ multiplies the total by about seven, and extending the integral to z = 12 adds about 65%.7 The fitted faint-end slopes (α) range from about −1.0 to −2.1. When α approaches −2 the number of galaxies keeps climbing as you go to lower masses, so the cutoff, rather than the data, sets the total.
  • It is a count across time, not a snapshot. Looking out in space means looking back in time, so the total adds up galaxies seen at every epoch out to z = 8. The study found that the number of galaxies per unit volume has fallen by roughly a factor of ten since the early universe, from about 0.7 per cubic megaparsec at z = 8, because small galaxies have merged into larger ones.7,8
  • “90% unseen” is a press-release summary. NASA's 2016 release says 90% of galaxies are too faint and too far for current telescopes.8 The paper itself gives detected and total surface densities of about 1,600 and about 10,000 galaxies per arcmin² at Hubble depth.7 That is roughly three galaxies per square arcsecond, so nearly every line of sight eventually ends on a galaxy.

What Counts as a Galaxy?

If the total depends on the faintest systems, the definition of a galaxy matters. The most widely used working definition comes from Willman and Strader: “a gravitationally bound collection of stars whose properties cannot be explained by a combination of baryons and Newton's laws of gravity”. In practice that means signs of dark matter, or a spread in heavy-element content showing that stars formed over a long period.9 By that test, Segue 1, a system of only a few hundred Suns' worth of light with a mass-to-light ratio near 3,400, is a galaxy. Omega Centauri, which is far brighter, is catalogued as a globular cluster, and some ultra-compact dwarfs remain ambiguous.9

Physics also sets a floor. Gas can only cool and form stars efficiently in dark-matter halos of roughly 5 × 10⁷ M☉ at z ≈ 10.10 Once the universe was reionized, simulations suggest the smallest halos able to host galaxies were around 3 × 10⁸ M☉, and only halos above about 5 × 10⁹ M☉ reliably contain one. Below that sit starless “RELHICs”: halos holding gas but no stars.11 In 2025 astronomers reported the first strong example, Cloud-9. It has about a million solar masses of hydrogen in a halo of about five billion solar masses, with no detectable stars down to a limit of 10⁴ M☉.12 Objects like it are failed galaxies, and no galaxy count would include them.

The same uncertainty applies to our own galaxy's surroundings. Models predict roughly 220 ± 50 satellite galaxies of the Milky Way above current detection limits.13 Other models predict as many as about 440.14 The 2026 DELVE census counts 49 known satellites above its completeness threshold and predicts 265 (+79/−47) in total.15 Most of our own galactic neighbours have not yet been found.

Weighing the Dark: Background Light

Nov 2026 There is a second, independent way to check the total: measure all the light that galaxies have ever emitted and compare it with the light from galaxies already catalogued. From the inner solar system this is nearly impossible because sunlight scattered off interplanetary dust (zodiacal light) swamps the signal. NASA's New Horizons probe, more than 50 AU from the Sun, can make the measurement.

  • 2021: Using archival LORRI images, Lauer et al. measured a cosmic optical background of about 16–19 nW m⁻² sr⁻¹, roughly twice the light from known galaxies.16 The press coverage read this as evidence for “hundreds of billions,” not trillions, of galaxies. NASA later issued a correction: the data “do not place a constraint on the total number of galaxies” but on the total light they emit.17
  • 2022: A dedicated observation found 16.37 ± 1.47 nW m⁻² sr⁻¹. That left an “anomalous” 8.06 ± 1.92 unaccounted for, as much again as all known galaxies.18
  • 2024: Postman et al. observed 16 fields and used Planck maps to correct for starlight scattered by dust in our own galaxy. They measured 11.16 ± 1.65 nW m⁻² sr⁻¹, against 8.17 ± 1.18 expected from known galaxies, leaving a residual of 2.99 ± 2.03, which is consistent with zero. In their words, the “simplest interpretation” is that the background is entirely due to galaxies.19

Gamma rays point the same way. High-energy photons from distant blazars are absorbed when they collide with background starlight on the way to us, so the amount of dimming acts as a measure of that light. Fermi-LAT used 739 blazars and one gamma-ray burst to reconstruct cosmic star formation over 90% of cosmic history, finding a peak near z ≈ 2.21 A 2026 update with 1,576 blazars detects the effect at about 23σ, consistent with galaxy counts and with New Horizons (preprint).22 Deep galaxy counts also converge: the faintest galaxies add less than 5–10% to the optical background, even though they are the most numerous.20

The lesson is that faint galaxies are numerous but contribute little light. The background light is now accounted for, but it cannot tell 300 billion galaxies from 2 trillion, because the extra dwarfs would add almost nothing to it. One puzzle remains: New Horizons ultraviolet data still show more far-UV background than galaxies appear to supply. Two 2026 preprints attribute only about 25–30% of it to extragalactic light.23

The Edge of the Census: JWST

Nov 2026 JWST has pushed the census back to within 300 million years of the Big Bang. The current spectroscopic record is MoM-z14 at z = 14.44, seen about 280 million years after the Big Bang.28 Its discovery team estimates that bright galaxies like it are about 182 times (+329/−105) more common than pre-JWST models predicted.29 The previous record holder, JADES-GS-z14-0, has been pinned by ALMA to z = 14.1793.30

That excess of early galaxies has limits. A 2026 search of more than 0.6 deg² of JWST imaging (McLeod et al., preprint) finds galaxy density about 12 times lower above z ≈ 12.5 than at 9.5–12.5. Only two candidates lie beyond z ≈ 14.5, and none at z ≥ 16.31 An October 2026 community review puts the decline at about sevenfold between z = 9 and z = 14. It lists bursty star formation, low dust, higher star-formation efficiency, a top-heavy initial mass function and hidden black holes as possible explanations.32 These bright early galaxies are a tiny fraction of the total. Whether the early universe made more galaxies or simply made some of them brighter sooner is still open.

The edge of the census also attracts impostors. “Capotauro,” a candidate galaxy at z ≈ 32, turned out in 2026 to be a Milky Way brown dwarf about 730 parsecs away at roughly 350 K. Its motion across the sky, about 37.6 milliarcseconds per year, gave it away.33 Other JWST sources are hard to classify: the compact “little red dots” number in the hundreds (341 in one compilation), and it is unclear whether they are galaxies, black holes or both.34

Reionization offers a third, indirect census. Lensed fields show the galaxy luminosity function still rising steeply (α ≈ −2) down to very faint galaxies (UV magnitude about −13) at z ≈ 9–15. Galaxies fainter than about −16 may produce more than half of all star formation at z ≈ 12.24 Dwarf galaxies also emit ionizing photons efficiently; Atek et al. measured about four times the usual rate.25 Taken at face value, these galaxies produce more ionizing photons than reionization needs, a tension called the “photon budget crisis.”26 A proposed 2025 resolution invokes clumpy gas and escape fractions near 5% (preprint).27 Either way, the faint majority must be there.

Nov 2026 JWST goes deep over small areas, and the new wide surveys cover large areas. Together they will improve the statistics that every total-count extrapolation depends on. Even the largest of them will catalogue only a few percent of the estimated total.

Nov 2026 Dark Energy Complication — DESI 2025–2026

DESI's March 2025 analysis used nearly 15 million galaxies and quasars covering 11 billion years. It suggested dark energy may have been stronger in the past and is now weakening. Depending on which external data (cosmic microwave background, supernovae) were combined, the preference ranged from 2.8 to 4.2 sigma, below the 5-sigma threshold for a discovery.46 The picture remains unsettled. DESI finished its planned five-year map in April 2026 with more than 47 million galaxies and quasars, and full-survey results are expected in 2027.47 A July 2026 Lyman-alpha analysis also moved back toward the standard model.52 Dark energy matters for this question because the expansion history converts what we observe into volumes and epochs. Changing it changes every extrapolation from the galaxies we see to the ones we don't.

A Number That Changes With Time

The count is not fixed even in principle. Because cosmic expansion is accelerating, there is a cosmic event horizon about 16 billion light-years away. Light that galaxies beyond about z ≈ 1.8 emit today will never reach us, even though we still see their older light. The observable universe itself extends about 46 billion light-years.49 Over the very long term, within roughly 10¹¹ years, every galaxy outside our gravitationally bound Local Group will fade from view. Their images effectively freeze at the ages we last saw them.50 For a few hundred billion years, then, the answer to “how many galaxies?” is mostly a question about the observer.

For scale, ESA estimates the number of stars in the observable universe at between 10²² and 10²⁴.51
